**Edition:** Network / Community Infrastructure

> **Evidence in silicon. Context in the network. Authority with communities.**

This publication brings the complete **selfdriven.network Silicon Kit v0.1** documentation together in one place. The companion ZIP contains executable source code, test vectors, an offline interface explorer and an educational RTL counter.

**Primary scope:** hardware-backed evidence and community policy at the boundary between physical resources and the published selfdriven.network interfaces.

# 01 — Purpose and framing

## What the kit is

The **selfdriven.network Silicon Kit** is a practical proposal for linking physical systems to a community-oriented network. It starts with evidence generated by a device, then adds community-controlled rules, identity and context, before any intelligent or economic action is permitted.

The aim is **community self-actuation**, not another general-purpose chip or proprietary platform. It should allow an individual, a cooperative or an independent Octo to work with real-world infrastructure while retaining meaningful human accountability.

Three separations matter:

1. **Silicon supplies evidence**: a protected key may sign a statement; counters and secure boot may strengthen device provenance.
2. **Network supplies interpretation**: entities, contexts, permissions, connections, evidence policy and coordination reside in network interfaces and data.
3. **Communities supply legitimacy**: who is allowed to assert something, how to challenge it, and what consequences follow are governance choices.

## What the kit is not

This is not a claim that the Foundation has fabricated an ASIC, that an experimental signer is tamper-proof, or that a local policy file is a valid credential. It is not a system for turning signed claims directly into payments, licences or safety actions. The proposed interfaces here are not represented as deployed APIs.

## Outcomes

- A portable **hardware-to-network evidence contract** independent of a single chip or blockchain.
- Community enrolment, suspension, key rotation and revocation as first-class concerns.
- Data minimisation: share a proof or derived fact when raw data is unnecessary.
- Offline-first capture with later verification and sync, where suitable.
- Clear assurance distinctions: software key, protected secure element, attested firmware, validated sensor, corroborated event.
- Independent verification of evidence and an inspectable chain of reasoning for community decisions.

## 4Cs applied to silicon

**Curious**: publish explanations and experiments. **Caring**: avoid mass telemetry and unconsented identifiers. **Constructive**: produce concrete interoperable artefacts. **Chill**: make entry possible with a laptop before a chip foundry.

## Primary audiences

Community infrastructure operators, makers, educators, governance designers, open-silicon collaborators, interface maintainers, and developers working on Octonomous, selfdrivenOS and physical-world verification.

## First question

What physical activity would a community genuinely benefit from verifying — and which facts would remain uncertain even after cryptographic verification?

# 02 — Network and silicon architecture

## Existing public network direction

The published selfdriven.network website describes eight interfaces: **Human, Social, Entity, Attachment, SSI, AI, OnChain and Infra**. It also describes an **Interfaces Node** initiative centred on SSI, Core/artefacts, Attachment, AI, OnChain and Infra. Its production API pages use HTTP POST with JSON request/response patterns and application authentication. The present kit does **not** assume it has access to any production API.

## Logical layers

```text
  PEOPLE + COMMUNITIES                OCTOS + APPLICATIONS
  Human / Social / governance         authorised assistance
              \                             /
               \                           /
            [Community policy and intent]
                           |
          +----------------+------------------+
          |        selfdriven.network         |
          | Entity/Core: artefacts, context   |
          | SSI: identity and credentials     |
          | Attachment: documents/evidence    |
          | AI: interpret, advise, orchestrate |
          | OnChain: assets, optional anchors  |
          | Infra: physical/virtual resource  |
          +----------------+------------------+
                           |
           [proposed silicon-evidence adapter]
                           |
           [event verification + policy check]
                           |
                  [gateway / MCU host]
                           |
              [hardware protected signing]
                           |
                  [sensor / actuator]
```

## The critical boundaries

**Boundary A: physical to digital.** The sensor and its environment can be wrong, manipulated or misleading. Cryptography does not correct them. Calibration, cross-checking and provenance matter.

**Boundary B: host to signer.** The host can be compromised. Signing policy and secure boot become important when signing has consequences. A generic secure element may secure a key without proving what software composed the message.

**Boundary C: signer to network.** A signature proves control of a cryptographic key over exact bytes. Separately determine device enrolment, community membership, purpose, permitted action and current key status.

**Boundary D: context to action.** A VCG projection or AI interpretation is not itself authorisation. Safety-critical or economically material acts require independent rules and approvals.

## A proposed integration message

The runnable kit creates an illustrative envelope:

```json
{
  "proposal": "selfdriven.network.silicon.bridge.v0.1",
  "interface": "Infra -> Entity/Core / SSI / Attachment / AI / OnChain",
  "transport": "local-mock-only",
  "method": "ingest-signed-evidence",
  "data": { "communityId": "community:solar-commons", "event": "<signed event>" },
  "productionApiCompatibility": "not-established"
}
```

The above is **not** a real documented selfdriven.network method. Its fields are candidate interface design inputs only.

## Data movement

1. The host receives a sample from an explicitly identified sensor source.
2. The signer signs canonicalised event bytes and a domain prefix.
3. An independent verifier checks signature, key binding, sequence and prior hash.
4. A community policy engine examines the enrolment and allowed evidence type.
5. A proposed context-graph projection links device, community and observation without declaring the observation true.
6. Optionally, a batch commitment is prepared for third-party timestamp/anchoring workflows; the example does not submit a transaction.
7. An Octo or human may interpret the result, but the authority to act remains constrained by separate policy.

## Design rule

Treat an **observation**, **signed claim**, **verified signature**, **enrolled device**, **attested hardware**, **validated measurement** and **authorised decision** as different data types, not interchangeable labels.

# 03 — Mapping to the eight selfdriven interfaces

| Published interface | Role in a silicon-enabled network | Proposed integration, not yet confirmed |
|---|---|---|
| Human | Identity, participation and meaningful human approval | View evidence, consent, challenge, governance dashboard |
| Social | Distributed social identity and discoverability | Optional community affiliation, not a source of device truth |
| Entity | Communities, resources, relationships and organisational activity | Community asset record, device registration, activity linkage |
| Attachment | Manage referenced evidence artefacts | Store source files or supporting calibration documents, with access rules |
| SSI | Device and human identity, credentials, presentations | Issued device operator credentials; issuer trust and revocation |
| AI | Contextual interpretation and autonomous assistance | Octo consumes only labelled, verified evidence and explains uncertainty |
| OnChain | Optional public commitments, accounts and transactions | Batch hash anchor without raw identifying evidence |
| Infra | Compute, storage, telecom, sensors, physical and virtual nodes | Device gateway, secure element, node status, resource measurement |

The **Interfaces Node** concept also identifies **Core** as a foundational artefact capability. In this kit, Core is treated as the authoritative home for community context and linked artefacts, subject to the real implementation's access rules. This terminology is a mapping proposal, not a replacement for existing interface specifications.

## Application: community energy commons

A community runs a small energy asset, with three device roles:

- **Meter**: measures energy. Its calibration, reference measurements and tamper seals determine physical evidence quality.
- **Host node**: obtains readings and composes events. It can lose connectivity and later synchronise a queue.
- **Signing device**: protects a signing key and authenticates events. Its assurance depends on provisioning and hardware verification.

On the network, an Entity/Core artefact represents the asset and its governance. SSI can bind an operator or device credential after approval. Attachment can link calibration certificates. Infra reports capability and health. AI can propose a maintenance action. Humans or authorised community processes decide whether to act. OnChain can record a compact public commitment if appropriate.

## Alternative pilots

**Environmental stewardship:** capture temperature, water-quality or air-sensor evidence; do not treat an uncalibrated device as a regulatory measurement.

**Shared resource exchange:** attest use of a community workshop resource; enforce privacy and fair access before any reputation or reward system.

**Octonomous task executor:** hardware-backed keys for a local Octo operating actuator requests, with explicit capability limits, audit records and an independent emergency stop.

**Distributed network node:** attest firmware/software measurements and heartbeat provenance, then report into a community-operated infrastructure catalogue. A heartbeat is not proof of service quality.

## A first non-negotiable decision

Agree which existing interface owns device enrolment and revocation, and which interface stores the authoritative version of the policy. This kit uses unsigned JSON mock files until that is decided.

# 04 — Identity, governance and trust

## Four independently evaluated claims

| Claim | Example test | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Cryptographic validity | Ed25519 signature matches exact bytes | Device honesty or ownership |
| Community authorisation | An approved registry says device can submit this event type | Registry authenticity unless secured |
| Hardware provenance | Manufacturer/owner attestation chain and measured firmware | Sensor truthfulness or proper governance |
| Observation confidence | Calibrated sensor, corroboration, recorded conditions | Automatically authorised action |

For v0.1, only the first row is implemented cryptographically. The second is simulated through **unsigned local policy**. Hardware provenance and observation confidence are **not implemented**.

## Device identity versus personal identity

The educational identifier `sdid:spki256:...` is a key fingerprint based on the public key's SubjectPublicKeyInfo DER bytes. It is **not** a W3C DID method, not a passport, and not a human identifier. Future SSI mapping should avoid copying persistent device IDs into unnecessary public contexts or creating an invisible surveillance graph.

A community may choose privacy-preserving, purpose-specific credentials that disclose eligibility without exposing a permanent global device history. Actual credential syntax and verification should be selected with the SSI maintainers.

## Lifecycle proposal

1. **Prepare:** operator creates a new protected device key and securely records provisioning evidence.
2. **Enrol:** authorised community reviewers approve device purpose and evidence types.
3. **Operate:** record signed observations with bounded rates and least privilege.
4. **Review:** renew authorisation, firmware posture and sensor quality checks.
5. **Suspend:** temporary restriction for anomalies or lost custody.
6. **Revoke:** remove authorisation and prevent future acceptance.
7. **Rotate:** issue a new key, preserving authorised continuity without asserting keys are equivalent.
8. **Retire:** dispose of hardware, purge keys, and apply retention policy.

## Community governance questions — 5W1H

- **Why:** what outcome does this infrastructure serve, and what are the least harmful alternatives?
- **Who:** members, operators, issuers, verifiers, reviewers and independent challengers.
- **What:** assets, evidence classes, permitted uses, stewardship duties and resources.
- **How:** quorum, appeals, calibration, audit, key recovery and incident escalation.
- **Where:** locality, physical custody, jurisdiction and data-residency conditions.
- **When:** intervals, expiry, review points and incident response windows.

## Proposed roles

**Community steward** approves operating policy; **device custodian** is responsible for hardware; **credential issuer** endorses role or equipment status; **independent verifier** checks bytes, certificates and expiry; **auditor** can review activity and disputes; **Octo** may assist within a revocable, bounded capability. None of these roles should automatically be the device manufacturer's role.

## Key management boundaries

For the first secure-element pilot, verify whether the selected chip supports non-exportable Ed25519 or another approved algorithm, documented firmware provisioning, attestation, secure update and compromise recovery. A product being labelled a secure element does not guarantee every capability or implementation is suitable.

# 05 — Evidence profile and Verified Context Graph proposal

## Inherited signed-event protocol

This kit deliberately retains the earlier educational `selfdriven.silicon.evidence.v0.1` format so network adapters can be explored without redefining cryptography. Each event includes:

- A public-key-derived **device ID** (not an asserted human identity).
- A monotonic **sequence** and `previousHash` pointing to the prior event.
- An ISO timestamp supplied by the untrusted host clock.
- A single supported educational type `energy.production.sample`.
- `observation` values: source ID, watt-hours and interval seconds.
- A `proof` with public key, Ed25519 signature and SHA-256 event hash.

The event is signed over `"selfdriven.silicon.evidence.v0.1\n"` plus a narrow deterministic JSON encoding (alphabetically sorted ASCII object keys, safe integer numbers). This is **not RFC 8785 JCS**, not a broad interoperability claim, and is intentionally insufficient for production standardisation.

## Verification sequence

1. Reject extra or missing keys and unsupported field types.
2. Check protocol prefix and key-derived device identity.
3. Recompute canonical bytes and SHA-256 event hash.
4. Verify Ed25519 over the same exact bytes.
5. Check event sequence and previous-hash linkage.
6. Evaluate **separate** community registry and policy.
7. Record whether attestation, calibration and trusted time were independently established. Here they were not.
8. Generate a graph projection only after success; do not automatically publish it.

## A minimal context graph proposal

```text
[community:solar-commons]        [sdid:spki256:...]
          ^                                |
          | listedByLocalRegistry          | signed
          |                                v
          +---------------------- [evidence:sha256:...]
                                      |
                                  observation
                                      |
                         simulated energy counter
```

The JSON proposal in `examples/vcg-projection.json` includes nodes and edges with an explicit `proposed-uncommitted` status and caveats. It does not imply that a production VCG schema or authoritative context record exists. A live integration would map these concepts into the actual Entity/Core and SSI models and record the verification policy version.

## Evidence confidence vocabulary

**signed:** signature checks. **locally-permitted:** unsigned local registry and policy accept it. **hardware-attested:** independently verified hardware/firmware chain, not in v0.1. **physically-corroborated:** external checks of real-world facts, not in v0.1. **context-validated:** a community-approved model of what the evidence means. **decision-authorised:** a separate action approval, not supplied by the evidence itself.

## Storage and privacy

The default sample is public, simulated, and contains no personal measurements. A real deployment should encrypt sensitive attachments, minimise device linkability, define retention periods, validate data ownership and attach machine-readable access restrictions. Avoid storing raw sensor streams or personal identity data on a public blockchain merely because an event is signed.

# 06 — Cardano, anchors and accountable action

## Why optional anchoring might help

A blockchain can offer public ordering and long-term commitment evidence for a *small digest* of off-chain records. It cannot prove whether an environmental or energy reading is accurate, whether a device was authorised, or whether a governance decision was legitimate.

A community could periodically publish a digest of a verified evidence batch, leaving detailed records in community storage and providing selective proof to legitimate reviewers.

## What v0.1 does

`src/network.js` creates a **local anchor manifest** containing:

```json
{
  "format": "selfdriven.network.anchor-manifest.v0.1",
  "communityId": "community:solar-commons",
  "evidenceCount": 3,
  "evidenceHead": "sha256:<last-event-hash>",
  "digest": "sha256:<batch-digest>",
  "chain": "none",
  "status": "not-submitted"
}
```

The `digest` is SHA-256 over deterministically encoded `domain`, `communityId` and the ordered event-hash list. This is a **demo-specific commitment**, not a Merkle root. Once published, a real design must specify byte canonicalisation, uniqueness, chain/reorg policy, record availability, verifier interoperability and versioning.

## Future Cardano flow

1. Independently verify the batch and community approvals.
2. Compute a stable batch digest using an agreed encoding.
3. Build a Cardano transaction with the selected metadata label or application protocol.
4. Sign via a wallet with appropriate custody and explicit authorisation.
5. Submit, monitor inclusion, wait for appropriate confirmation depth.
6. Store transaction ID, network, slot and applied policy as separate evidence.
7. Offer independent inclusion and mapping verification.

Cardano transaction metadata supports compact CBOR-encoded structured data under integer metadata labels. It should hold **hashes and minimal metadata**, not personal data, device private keys or raw measurements.

## Octos and governance

An Octo can compare measurements against community goals, flag anomalies or draft a decision. It should not obtain implicit transfer authority, claim ownership or automatically issue rewards because an input is signed. Capability boundaries, human review, dispute processes and economic settlement proofs are separate protocol concerns.

## Avoid premature tokenisation

Start with integrity and usable data. Do not assume a signed energy reading is an accepted unit of value, regulatory-grade proof, a carbon credit, or a basis for OCTO issuance. Incentives can distort sensors and reporting; assess adverse behaviour before offering economic rewards.

# 07 — Silicon, secure elements and open-PDK path

## Build with existing hardware first

The highest-value early question is not whether a custom chip can be fabricated; it is whether a community-backed signer can reliably support the chosen protocol with understandable lifecycle and governance.

**Prototype components** (indicative, not a bill of materials or a purchase commitment):

| Component | Purpose | Selection question |
|---|---|---|
| Raspberry Pi or MCU host | Interface with sensor, network and signer | Can it queue offline and support secure updates? |
| Secure element evaluation kit | Non-exportable signing or key isolation | Which algorithms, attestation chain and firmware versions are verifiable? |
| Simulated energy meter | Repeatable software demonstration | Can a real calibrated sensor later replace it? |
| Local NodeJS adapter | Verify signatures, enforce mocked policy | Is it decoupled from vendor protocols? |
| Community admin interface | Approve devices and revoke authorisation | Who has authority, evidence and appeal rights? |

**TROPIC01** is a promising research candidate: Tropic Square publishes technical documentation, development boards, firmware and RTL resources. Its integration still requires detailed review of algorithms, provisioning, threat model and SDK capabilities. **OpenTitan** provides an open root-of-trust design and documentation for creator/owner attestation concepts. It is not a drop-in replacement for TROPIC01, and using either does not automatically confer hardware security on this demo.

## Chip design vocabulary

- **RTL:** behavioural hardware logic expressed in Verilog/SystemVerilog or similar languages.
- **PDK:** the foundry's process design kit with device models, design rules and layout constraints.
- **EDA flow:** tools for synthesis, placement, routing, extraction, timing and physical verification.
- **ASIC:** a fabricated application-specific chip, after design, validation, sign-off and packaging.
- **MPW:** shared multi-project wafer run, suitable for experimental circuits within constraints.

## Three stages

**Stage A — software:** reproduce signatures, hash-chain verification and community revocation tests on a laptop. Use this kit.

**Stage B — real secure hardware:** add a verified signer interface to a host with protected keys, secure provisioning, real firmware lifecycle and a sensor. Independently verify attestations where the hardware supports them.

**Stage C — open silicon:** evaluate an open RISC-V subsystem, suitable security IP and an open PDK such as SKY130 or GF180MCU. Begin with an educational event counter and basic bus logic. A hardened secure element requires professional review, resistance to physical attacks, secure entropy and process-specific assurance; a tiny tapeout does not demonstrate those properties.

## Signer interface contract proposal

```js
// Prototype-only shape, not an established selfdriven.network API
const signer = {
  publicKeySpki: 'base64url(DER SPKI)',
  deviceId: 'sdid:spki256:...',
  signerType: 'software-demo',
  sign(messageBytes) { /* return Buffer signature */ }
};
```

At the hardware migration point, isolate the signer as an asynchronous adapter if needed (`.then()`-friendly), and keep private keys off the host. Distinguish `sign()` success from attestation of firmware and from permission to cause effects.

## Custom silicon decision gate

A dedicated chip only becomes justified after a credible field pilot shows material constraints that cannot be met by existing secure elements or processors: power, cost at volume, tamper-resistance, supply-chain transparency, lifecycle controls or unique verifiable functionality. Until then, openness is better delivered at the **interface and verification** layers than by asserting an unvalidated custom security chip.

# 08 — Threat model, safety and privacy

## What must be protected

Device signing keys; community authorisation decisions; recorded observation integrity; operator privacy; evidence storage; update and revocation pathways; any permission to operate physical assets; and the availability of the Interfaces Node.

## Threats and candidate mitigations

| Threat | What goes wrong | Mitigation / design gate |
|---|---|---|
| Compromised host | Host signs false observations using available signer | Authenticated sensor, strict signer policy, firmware measurement and corroboration |
| Forged registry | Attacker adds their device to allowlist | Signed issuer-approved policy, robust access control and audit trail |
| Key theft | Counterfeit device signs events | Non-exportable keys, provisioning controls, revocation and detection |
| Replay | Old events accepted as new activity | Persistent dedupe, sequence windows, challenge-response and epoch markers |
| Counter reset | New chain masks gaps | Trusted counter or signed reset/rotation event, continuity policy |
| Fake time | Host lies about measurement timestamp | Trusted time or external timestamp attestation where necessary |
| Malicious sensor | Perfectly signed wrong physical data | Calibration, independent comparison, provenance and audits |
| Public correlation | Device history reveals private behaviour | Rotating/purpose-limited credentials, access control, selective disclosure |
| DoS or availability loss | Community cannot verify or act | Offline queues, independent replicas, rate limits, safe operation modes |
| AI overreach | Octo executes unsafe instruction based on uncertain data | Capability-scoped actions, independent guards, review and kill switch |
| On-chain data leakage | Permanent sensitive identifiers become public | Hash commitments, minimal metadata, no private data on-chain |

## Implemented versus not implemented

**Implemented in the local demo:** Ed25519 signature check, chain sequence, simple policy allowlist and revocation behaviour, process-memory replay detection for a localhost endpoint, local manifest digest matching.

**Not implemented:** authenticated registry/policy updates, production enrolment, persistent replay database, secure element integration, remote attestation, TLS/API authentication, certificate issuance, tamper-resistant sensing, trusted time, access control over graph projections or real-world actuation.

## Safe-default rules

- Bind the demo server to `127.0.0.1`, never `0.0.0.0` by default.
- Never paste real API credentials, private keys, wallet mnemonics or personal readings into example JSON.
- Never let an Octo trigger an actuator or spend money from a single signed observation.
- Treat all experimental trust indicators as *demonstration only*.
- Treat the site’s mention of ISO standards as architectural context, not evidence that this new kit or node is certified.

## Assurance rubric

**L0 — simulated:** software key and synthetic readings. **L1 — protected key:** secure element with tested key protection. **L2 — attested platform:** validated provenance of approved firmware and device. **L3 — corroborated observation:** measurements supported by calibrated, independent evidence. **L4 — governance-authorised:** documented decision rights, current policy and audit/appeal. These are **proposed local labels**, not an industry standard; levels are not automatically cumulative or universal.

# 09 — Hands-on learning labs

## Lab 1 — First simulated community node (20 minutes)

Run `npm run demo`. Read the generated `examples/evidence.json`. Identify `deviceId`, sequence, hash, signature, energy reading and `observedAt`. Observe that keys are not saved, the readings are synthetic and every new run creates a new identity.

**Outcome:** explain why a signed event is different from a verified physical measurement.

## Lab 2 — Independent verification (15 minutes)

Run `npm run verify`. In a copy of `examples/evidence.json`, change one `wattHours` field; run `node src/verify.js <changed-file> ...`. The signature must fail. Restore and reorder events; the hash chain must fail.

**Outcome:** separate byte integrity from sequencing integrity.

## Lab 3 — Community autonomy (15 minutes)

Change `status` in `examples/community-registry.json` from `active` to `revoked`. Run verify again. The cryptographic chain should still pass, while community permission is denied.

**Outcome:** governance can change an authorisation without changing past cryptographic facts.

## Lab 4 — Network context (20 minutes)

Read `examples/vcg-projection.json`. Identify Community, DeviceIdentity and SignedObservation nodes and their relationships. Propose an additional attachment or issuer credential, keeping provenance and uncertainty explicit.

**Outcome:** distinguish a graph projection from an authoritative community fact.

## Lab 5 — Local API bridge and replay (20 minutes)

Run `npm run serve`. POST the example bundle to `/demo/ingest` using the README command. Send it again: the server rejects duplicate event hashes in memory. Restart the server and repeat: replay state is reset, exposing the need for persistent distributed replay protection.

**Outcome:** understand the difference between a local mock and a production Interfaces Node endpoint.

## Lab 6 — A Cardano commitment without a transaction (20 minutes)

Open `examples/anchor-manifest.json`. Explain `status: not-submitted`, the ordered event hash digest and what would be required for actual inclusion and confirmation. Never infer chain finality from local output.

**Outcome:** describe the narrow role of public anchoring.

## Lab 7 — Read the hardware path (45–60 minutes)

Open `rtl/event_counter.sv`. Simulate with a suitable SystemVerilog simulator if available, such as Icarus Verilog. Explain why a digital event counter does not provide secure key custody, randomness, side-channel resistance, firmware attestation or sensor integrity.

**Outcome:** understand how a PDK relates to physical fabrication without confusing educational RTL with a finished secure chip.

## Lab 8 — Community deployment design workshop (60 minutes)

Define a real asset, trusted measurement source, operators, revocation policy, privacy boundaries, evidence retention, safety fallback and review process. Record five decisions and three unresolved risks. Do not connect live assets until all gates are met.

**Outcome:** a governance-aware pilot design, not just a working signing function.

# 10 — Roadmap, success criteria and decision gates

## Phase 0 — Kit validation (provided)

**Deliverable:** a local, repeatable signature/registry/policy demonstration, proposed context graph and offline anchor manifest. **Proof:** automated tests and reproducible example generation. **Caveat:** software-only, locally controlled trust inputs.

## Phase 1 — Network interface alignment

**Target:** agree with interface maintainers on how an enrolled physical device is represented. Specify data ownership, schema, keys, versioned field naming, permission checks and where context graphs live. Select explicit APIs rather than adding undefined methods to production endpoints.

**Gate:** a reviewed interface contract, independent implementation and operational data-retention agreement.

## Phase 2 — Protected hardware pilot

**Target:** one community-owned device and protected key backed by an evaluated secure element, plus at least one independently validated sensor or test signal.

**Gate:** documented provisioning, recovery, remote attestation (if supported), software update, revocation and field-test traceability.

## Phase 3 — Community-operated node

**Target:** durable store-and-forward, independent verifier replicas, signed policy/registry, monitoring and human governance. Avoid systemic reliance on a single central oracle.

**Gate:** tests for loss of connectivity, replay across restarts, key rotation, compromised host, mistaken attribution and transparent dispute resolution.

## Phase 4 — Optional public commitment

**Target:** Cardano anchor for selected batches, after privacy and data value are justified. Define finality and audit evidence.

**Gate:** independent on-chain inclusion verification, fee/custody budget, privacy review and legal assessment for consequences.

## Phase 5 — Open ASIC exploration

**Target:** evaluate PDK, security IP, design verification, fabrication access, packaging, lifecycle and costs. May never be necessary.

**Gate:** clear value over commodity trusted hardware; independent security and economic review.

## MVP acceptance checklist

- [x] Every signed event is independently verifiable offline.
- [x] Tampering and out-of-order chains fail.
- [x] Revocation in local policy blocks new local approval.
- [x] A context-graph projection expresses provenance and uncertainty.
- [x] A local manifest can be independently checked; it claims no chain submission.
- [x] Local HTTP mock rejects duplicate hashes within one process.
- [ ] Registry and policy are governed and cryptographically authenticated.
- [ ] A real secure element is connected with independently verified attestation.
- [ ] A real sensor has appropriate calibration/corroboration.
- [ ] Interfaces Node production integration is reviewed and implemented.
- [ ] Distributed persistence, privacy and incident management have been tested.

## Candidate first six-week pilot

| Week | Milestone | Evidence produced |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Community use case and 5W1H decisions | Scope, risks, authority model |
| 2 | Review event schema + integration mapping | Contract draft and test vectors |
| 3 | Select host and secure-element kit | Hardware assessment and BOM |
| 4 | Provision and sign with protected key | Verification run and key lifecycle record |
| 5 | Add real sensor + policy lifecycle | Calibrated/corroborated test record |
| 6 | Independent audit + pilot decision | Findings, go/no-go and next backlog |

# 11 — Community stewardship, openness and decisions

## Why governance is technical

When evidence can influence access, value, reputation, resource allocation or action by an Octo, whoever controls enrolment and policy controls real outcomes. A cryptographically strong device should not obscure a weak democratic process.

## A proposed stewardship model

**Contributors:** publish and review open specifications, test vectors and reference code. **Community operators:** configure local enrolment, retention, calibration, fail-safe procedures and appeals. **Independent verifiers:** use the same public contract to reject malformed or unauthorised claims. **Auditors:** evaluate technical and community effects. **Maintainers:** publish security notices, deprecation rules and interoperability suites.

## Agreements to prepare before a field trial

- Who owns and may export the community evidence and its derivatives.
- Who can enrol, suspend, revoke or rotate a device identity.
- How data is consented, minimised, retained and deleted where possible.
- How communities handle disagreements between a signed record and reality.
- Whether AI output can ever invoke a physical device, and how authorisation is checked.
- How accessible and educational the tools should remain to operators.
- Which responsibilities are borne by the device maker, network host and community.

## Proposed technical licensing path

The kit intentionally declares `UNLICENSED` pending a contributor/owner decision. Before public release, choose appropriate open licences for **code**, **RTL**, **documentation** and **design assets**, and review compatibility with upstream libraries and secure-element hardware licences. An open PDK's individual licence does not automatically license an entire chip or design flow.

## First workshop decision register

| Decision | Suggested default | Owner to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| First physical proof | community renewable-energy observation | pilot community |
